From Blind Box to Brochure

An advocacy toolkit for a Singaporean youth non-profit: a blind box that unfolds into a brochure, plus a character keychain — designed to start conversations rather than end them.

Context

Kita is a Singaporean non-profit dedicated to helping youths claim their agency and build belonging, expression, and skills. Despite the impact of its work, it faces a strategic problem that threatens its long-term sustainability: a significant deficit in formal communication and advocacy infrastructure.

We targeted potential advocates and donors. Empowered by the financial capital and influential networks they hold, these individuals can amplify Kita's reach — a core tenet of stakeholder theory, which holds that an organisation's long-term viability depends on managing relationships with every group that supplies critical resources, not only the beneficiaries of its services (Freeman, 1984).

Kita's fundamental issue was not a lack of information, but a lack of means for starting conversations and sharing them.

Traditional brochures are one-way, terminal points of information. They run on an outdated diffusion model of communication, insufficient for motivating the organic, peer-to-peer advocacy Kita needs. Shifting toward participatory communication was what could turn passive sympathisers into active ambassadors (Servaes, 1996).

So the objective became: create a resource that acts not as a final statement but as a conversation starter — engaging, aesthetically compelling, easily shareable, and motivating enough that advocates champion Kita inside their own professional and social circles.

Research approach

Desk research and a board member

Extensive qualitative desk research across three themes — Kita's organisational context, Singapore's at-risk youth, and Singaporean advocate and donor profiles — plus one physical interview.

For organisational context we reviewed the websites, publications, and social channels of Kita, its predecessor Hatch, and comparable organisations. Using purposive sampling we selected Praxium and Trybe for their close alignment with Kita's work, then ran a SWOT analysis on each organisation's branding, programme presentation, and outreach. For donor profiles we reviewed public data on platforms such as GiveAsia alongside philanthropic trend reports, covering younger digitally-savvy donors, older established contributors, and volunteers and partners at varying engagement levels.

The interview was with Kwek Jim See, one of Kita's board members, to get first-hand insight from an existing advocate — the demographics, behaviour and preferences of advocates, and how information currently flows to potential ones.

Findings

Five findings, and what each meant for the design

1 — Awareness is thin outside existing networks

As a recently established non-profit with minimal press and social presence, Kita is largely unknown beyond its current supporters. So: the toolkit had to be a clear entry point — eye-catching and informative enough to spark and sustain conversations.

2 — Advocate profiles differ sharply

Donors split between younger people making online micro-donations and older donors giving larger, consistent amounts. Both Kita's team and Jim See flagged the need to tailor messaging to different motivations and familiarity with the social service landscape. So: visually engaging, interactive resources setting out several avenues for support.

3 — Youth-led agency is the differentiator

Research on at-risk youth showed their experiences stem from social and familial factors including intergenerational offending and educational pressure — which is why genuine involvement matters. Jim See reached for “agency” and “co-creation” when he wanted to catch someone's interest. So: foreground the collaborative ethos.

4 — Language choice is not cosmetic

Framing narratives around strength and potential rather than deficits or “sob stories” lets advocates communicate authentically without trading on pity. Kita's team specifically cautioned against “empower”, which implies the youths were powerless to begin with. So: language that spotlights space and opportunity, not rescue.

5 — There was no single grounded document

Both Kita's team and Jim See described the same gap: nowhere consolidated that brings together key information and clear ways to contribute. So: the toolkit had to be that document.

The intervention

A blind box that unfolds into a brochure

The toolkit is a brochure shaped as a blind box on the theme of home, plus an identity artefact — a character keychain with a matching card. Unfolded, the box becomes the brochure.

The keychain is modelled on Kita's four characters: Bubu, Roro, Kiki and Pebbie. Each comes with a card explaining the character's origins and what it represents about Kita. The idea came from Victor Zhu, Kita's executive director, who wears Kita's character pins to conferences to start conversations and leave an impression. Keychains were chosen for being conspicuous, attractive, and flexible in how they're displayed.

The blind box format carries Kita's visual identity into a medium that stands out among advocacy materials, reinforcing recognition and recall. The unboxing extends engagement past passive reading into something with emotional resonance — which is what makes the information stick.

Design process

Five constraints that shaped it

  1. 01

    Scalability

    Accessibility and visibility were the critical gaps, so the medium had to be cost-effective and easy to produce in bulk within a growing organisation's tight resources. The design is straightforward to assemble, which also simplifies inventory — and an interactive 3D brochure demonstrates the fold.

  2. 02

    Digestibility

    The cuboid gives twelve sides, which let us segregate information by proximity into clear categories. Visual hierarchy steers the eye toward critical information and clear calls to action, and the copy balances depth against skimmability.

  3. 03

    Portability

    Jim See described advocates carrying the toolkit around and using it when an opportunity arises — so it had to be compact and light enough that carrying it isn't a decision.

  4. 04

    Durability

    A blind box has to protect the keychain while being carried. A thicker material strengthens the structure and extends its life — and a box that stays in an advocate's space keeps generating touchpoints.

  5. 05

    Configurability

    Kita's mission centres on co-creation and youth agency, but we had no opportunity to collaborate with Kita's youths directly. So the design was made configurable, in the sense of infrastructuring (Dantec & DiSalvo, 2013): elements are deliberately left empty for youths to personalise. Rather than prescribing a finished solution, it provides a foundation — which turns beneficiaries into co-creators of how Kita is represented.

The balance it had to strike

Innovation against practical organisational needs, and youthfulness against professionalism — enough of the latter not to undermine the seriousness of the cause. The blind box is unconventional but culturally relevant, and its trendiness is doing work: it's built to be shared. What would otherwise be a generic brochure becomes a memorable first touchpoint.

Evaluation

How we'd measure it

Two outcomes: improve current advocates' knowledge of Kita and their confidence in communicating its mission, and drive measurable engagement — traffic to Kita's site and donation platforms.

Quantitatively, pre- and post-surveys for current advocates measuring knowledge and confidence before and after receiving the toolkit, and a single survey for new advocates covering perceived knowledge of Kita, frequency of advocacy activity, frequency of donation, and what motivates and sustains their interest. Alongside that, engagement metrics across Kita's social, website, and donation channels before and after distribution.

Qualitatively, field observation at Kita outreach events to see how advocates actually use the toolkit, and focus groups after a period of distribution to learn which elements help and which create friction. Tying both together, a longitudinal check at six months to a year to test whether any effect is sustained past the initial distribution.

Conclusion

The project shows how co-creation informed a communication toolkit built for Kita's emerging support ecosystem. Engaging directly with a representative of Kita's advocates clarified the motivations and interaction preferences that make advocacy work, and working closely with Kita's team kept organisational needs and design outcomes aligned.

Prototyping surfaced a real tension between meaningful storytelling and practical usability, which led to clearer instructions and more durable structural choices. Next would be testing with a broader advocate base and building in more participatory elements — user-driven customisation especially — to support sustained engagement and Kita's longer-term outreach goals.
