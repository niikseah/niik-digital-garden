From Blind Box to Brochure

An advocacy toolkit for Kita, a Singaporean youth non-profit: a blind box that unfolds into a brochure, packaged with character keychains — built to turn passive sympathisers into active ambassadors.

Context

Kita is a Singaporean non-profit dedicated to helping youths claim their agency and build belonging, expression, and skills. Despite the impact of the work, Kita faces a strategic threat to its long-term sustainability: a significant deficit in formal communication and advocacy infrastructure.

The problem

Kita's fundamental issue was never a lack of information — it was the lack of a means for starting conversations and sharing. Traditional brochures are one-way, terminal points of information, operating on a diffusion model too outdated to motivate the organic, peer-to-peer advocacy Kita needs.

Who we designed for

The toolkit targets potential advocates and donors. Empowered by the financial capital and influential networks they hold, these individuals can amplify Kita's reach — a core tenet of stakeholder theory, which holds that an organisation's long-term viability depends on managing relationships with every group supplying critical resources, not only its primary beneficiaries (Freeman, 1984).

The objective, then, was a resource that acts not as a final statement but as a conversation starter: engaging, aesthetically compelling, easily shareable, and capable of moving a sympathiser to champion Kita in their own professional and social circles (Servaes, 1996).

Research approach

  1. 01

    Desk research across three themes

    Kita's organisational context, Singapore's at-risk youth, and Singapore advocate and donor profiles — drawn from academic and non-academic sources.

  2. 02

    Competitive SWOT

    Reviewed the websites, publications, and social platforms of Kita, its predecessor Hatch, and — via purposive sampling — Praxium and Trybe, then ran a SWOT on each organisation's branding, programme presentation, and outreach.

  3. 03

    Donor profiling

    Analysed publicly available data on GiveAsia and reports on philanthropic trends, covering younger digitally-savvy donors, older established contributors, volunteers, and partners at varying engagement levels.

  4. 04

    Stakeholder interview

    Interviewed Kita board member Kwek Jim See to get first-hand insight into advocate demographics, behaviour, preferences, and the information flows between existing and potential advocates.

What the research found

  • Limited awareness. As a recently established non-profit with minimal press and social presence, Kita is largely unknown outside its existing networks — so the toolkit had to be an entry point eye-catching enough to start a conversation and informative enough to sustain it.
  • Advocate profiles differ. Younger donors prefer online micro-donations; older donors give larger, consistent amounts. Messaging had to flex across motivations and familiarity with the social service landscape.
  • Youth agency is the differentiator. Kita's participatory approach gives youths genuine involvement in shaping their own pathways — Jim See reached for “agency” and “co-creation” when sparking others' interest, so the collaborative ethos had to lead.
  • Language matters enormously. Framing narratives around strength and potential rather than deficits or “sob stories” lets advocates communicate authentically. Kita's team cautioned against “empower”, which implies the youths were powerless to begin with.
  • No consolidated entry point existed. Both Kita's team and Jim See named the need for a single, grounded document holding key information and clear ways to contribute.

The intervention

The toolkit is a brochure in the form of a blind box, themed around home, paired with an identity artefact: a character keychain and a matching card. Unfolded, the blind box becomes a brochure that can be read straight through.

The keychains are modelled on Kita's four characters — Bubu, Roro, Kiki, and Pebbie — each with a card explaining its origins and what it represents. The idea came from Kita's executive director, Victor Zhu, who wears Kita's character pins to conferences to start conversations. Keychains are conspicuous, attractive, and flexible in how they're displayed.

The blind box format carries Kita's visual identity into a medium unusual enough to stand out among advocacy materials, and the unboxing turns passive information consumption into an experience with emotional resonance — which is what makes it stick.

Design decisions

  • Scalability. A cost-effective medium producible in bulk, with a straightforward, easy-to-assemble design that streamlines assembly and inventory for an organisation working under tight resource constraints. An interactive 3D brochure demonstrates the fold.
  • Digestibility. The cuboid's 12 sides let information be segregated into clear categories by proximity, with a visual hierarchy steering readers toward the critical information and the calls to action. The copy balances depth against skimmability.
  • Portability. Advocates carry the toolkit and deploy it when an opportunity arises, so it had to be compact and lightweight enough that carrying it isn't a decision.
  • Durability. Thicker material protects the keychain in transit and extends the object's life — and a blind box still sitting in an advocate's space is a recurring touchpoint for further sharing.
  • Configurability. Elements are deliberately left empty so Kita's youths can personalise and adapt the toolkit — “infrastructuring” (Dantec & DiSalvo, 2013). Rather than prescribing a fixed solution, the design gives youths ownership of how Kita is represented, turning beneficiaries into co-creators of the advocacy narrative.

How we'd evaluate it

Two outcomes matter: that current advocates know Kita better and feel confident communicating its mission, and that engagement measurably rises — traffic to Kita's site and donation platforms.

The plan is mixed-method. Quantitatively: pre- and post-toolkit surveys with current advocates measuring knowledge and confidence, a survey of new advocates, and engagement metrics across social, web, and donation channels tracked before and after distribution. Qualitatively: field observation at Kita outreach events, watching how advocates actually use the toolkit in practice.
